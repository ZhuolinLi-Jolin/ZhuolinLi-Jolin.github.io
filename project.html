Final Project

An in-depth analytical exploration

Are AI Taking Our Jobs?

This project examines the link between AI adoption and occupational stability through a data-driven lens, addressing widespread automation fears. Unlike previous industrial shifts, Generative AI impacts distinct roles. Focusing on the United States, the frontier of AI innovation, we synthesize multi-dimensional datasets（including O*NET task data and BLS statistics) to visualize whether these technologies are merely replacing labor or driving a complex structural reorganization of the workforce.

Research Question

How is AI driving a structural shift in the US labor market?


To answer this question, this project draws upon the logic of labor economics. Rather than treating occupations as indivisible units, we deconstruct them into a bundle of specific tasks and abilities. This granularity is crucial because AI typically targets specific functions rather than entire job titles.


Simultaneously, this research introduces AI Exposure (AIOE) as a key analytical metric. This index measures the overlap between AI capabilities and the human abilities (such as logical reasoning and language comprehension) required for specific occupations. It represents the degree of potential impact that AI technology has on a particular profession, task, or industry. For example, the AIOE for painters is very low, whereas it is very high for translators.

AI Exposure (AIOE) and Abilities for Jobs

The AIOE framework typically utilizes the 52 human abilities defined by the U.S. Department of Labor's O*NET database. These abilities are categorized into four major groups. Among them, Cognitive Abilities exhibit the highest AIOE, while Physical Abilities are the lowest.The large bubble sizes indicate these are high-importance skills, meaning the "intellectual" core of the U.S. workforce is currently the most exposed to AI's impact.

AI Geographic Exposure (AIGE)


Furthermore, by integrating the industrial structure and occupational distribution of different regions across the United States, we can derive the Potential Extent of AI Technology Impact on the U.S. Regional Labor Market (AIGE).


Areas with higher AIGE offer the greatest potential for productivity gains but also face the strongest job market shocks if AI displacement accelerates too quickly; these disparities are stark even within states. For example, California’s Santa Clara County (2.247) far exceeds neighboring Monterey County (-1.943).

AI Exposure vs. Wage


The data reveals a strong positive correlation between annual mean wages and AI exposure scores, indicating a significant structural shift in today’s labor market. Unlike earlier waves of automation that mainly impacted routine manual work, recent AI developments are increasingly concentrated in higher-income sectors. This pattern is especially pronounced in Business, STEM, and Technology, where high-skill cognitive tasks face the greatest exposure. In contrast, manual and service-based occupations remain relatively less affected, likely due to the physical and contextual complexity of these roles.


The distribution of bubble sizes indicates that the total employment in high-exposure sectors is substantial, particularly within business and administrative roles. Given that many of the largest employment clusters coincide with high AI exposure scores, the potential for a broad-scale structural shift appears significant. This suggests that a cautious and proactive approach to workforce adaptation may be beneficial.

AI Exposure and Occupational Mix Change

We measure occupational mix change using a churn index, constructed following the data and methodology of The Budget Lab at Yale. Specifically, we develop an industry-level occupational structure change index based on the Duncan and Duncan (1955) dissimilarity index. Monthly deviations in occupational employment shares from a baseline month are computed and smoothed using a 12-month moving average, with higher values indicating greater occupational restructuring.


Occupational mix change is most pronounced in industries highly exposed to text-based and general-purpose AI, particularly information, finance, and professional services, while image-generative AI alone shows limited restructuring effects. This pattern suggests that contemporary AI-driven labor reallocation operates primarily through the reconfiguration of cognitive and analytical tasks rather than through visual or manual task substitution.

AI Exposure Score and Unemployment Composition by Age groups


Using CPS longitudinal microdata and AI exposure measures from Eloundou et al., this study classifies occupations into high, moderate, and low AI exposure based on the 2024 employment-weighted distribution, following Tyler et al. The sample is divided into young (20–24) and prime-age (25–55) workers across six groups, with 12-month moving averages applied to employment and transition rates to reduce noise; the chart focuses on high and low exposure cohorts.


After ChatGPT’s release, separation rates for young workers in high AI-exposure jobs fell initially but have recently rebounded, indicating possible AI-driven turnover or displacement. By contrast, young workers in low-exposure jobs and all prime-age workers have remained stable or declined slightly since 2022. The stability among ages 25–55 suggests that experience and seniority currently buffer against AI-related labor market shocks.

Key Findings

AI is driving a "top-down" structural shift by targeting high-wage, cognitive sectors, fueling geographic disparities in productivity and labor volatility. Rather than eliminating roles outright, AI accelerates occupational churn by addressing specific tasks within jobs—disproportionately affecting young workers while seniority buffers the prime-age workforce. While current AIOE metrics provide only a partial snapshot due to their inability to keep pace with the unprecedented speed of LLM evolution, it is this very rapid technological advancement that is driving shifts far beyond U.S. borders, signaling a universal labor paradigm. To navigate this inevitable transition, policymakers must move beyond debate to prioritize AI literacy and proactive workforce development, ensuring labor market resilience in an increasingly interconnected, AI-driven world.
